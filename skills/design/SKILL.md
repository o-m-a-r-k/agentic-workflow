# Screens that do their job

**The goal.** A person looks at the screen and knows, without working anything out, where they are, what is true, what matters most and what to do next, and can do it without fear of the wrong move. It looks like it belongs beside the product's best screens, and an experienced product designer would not change its structure; the passes under Practice are how that is judged. Fitting every width, following the design system and answering every question are part of that bar, not the bar.

## Principles

- **Purpose before layout.** A request to change what a screen says, other than correcting a typo, a translation or a notice the law or the money requires, is a question its user couldn't answer there; start from that question, not the words. Start from who uses it, in what situation, what they must come away knowing or doing, and the few questions they bring, in the order they bring them; that chooses what appears, in what order and in what form. How the data is stored or returned is never the layout: a screen that mirrors its payload, one field or one item per row, has skipped design.
- **Show, don't tell.** Each of the person's questions is answered by an element: a labelled value, a state, a mark, a control or a link. A sentence is a claim that can turn false in a state nobody pictured; it answers only where no element can carry the fact, and the design says why. The screen carries its meaning in what it shows: the state as a state, values side by side where they are compared, a difference marked where it is. Text that explains what the screen means, or tells the person what to do and where, is the design not yet done: the person has to read it, then hold it in mind. A sentence is a last resort for a fact that would otherwise be misread, one short line beside that fact, never a paragraph. When the person has something to do, the screen offers it there, or links to that action itself; it never narrates a procedure by the names of buttons elsewhere. When the action the person needs doesn't exist in the product, that is a product question for the person, never a sentence. When someone can't understand the screen, change what it shows; adding a sentence answers the reader and leaves the design as it was. Words that grow with each round are the design failing. The words an action answers with (an error, a confirmation of what can't be undone) and notices the law or the money requires are words by nature; they stay short and sit where they apply.
- **Importance is visible.** Each view has one focal point: the answer the person came for, and the next step. Size, weight, colour, position and space rank everything else beneath it. Things that look equally strong are read as equally important, so rank is designed, never left to the order the parts were built in.
- **Group by meaning, separate by space.** What belongs together sits together under one name; what doesn't stands apart. Space does this before lines and boxes do. A group's name says what its members have in common.
- **Like things look alike.** Items of the same kind get the same component, alignment, order and treatment everywhere on the screen. A different look means a different meaning; a difference with no meaning is noise.
- **Craft is part of meaning.** A few type sizes and weights, each with one job. Everything aligns to shared edges, and actions of one kind sit in one place. Spacing comes from the system's scale, with more space between groups than within them. Density fits the task: a tool used all day is compact and scannable, a rare task roomy and guided.
- **One clear next step.** The primary action is one, and it is the step the person most likely needs. Other actions are quieter and grouped together. Emphasis and colour rank actions and carry meaning, never decoration: danger styling only for what is destructive or irreversible, and where the brand colour reads as danger, secondary actions are neutral. What can't be done now appears only when knowing it changes what the person does, and then is shown by the state that prevents it, beside that state, not as a control or an explanation.
- **What it says and what it allows agree.** A warning, a status and the actions offered beside them tell one story in every state; a step the screen advises against is not presented as a ready choice.
- **Show the common, disclose the rare.** What most people need most of the time is on the surface; detail, technical facts and rare paths sit behind a clearly named disclosure. The answer to a main question is never hidden.
- **The person's words and formats.** Labels name what a thing means to them, not what the system calls it. One thing has one name everywhere, on the screen and across the product. A button names its outcome, not a generic verb. Values are formatted as the person would say them: money with its currency, dates in their calendar, states as words. Every word earns its place.
- **Every action answers.** Its result shows where the person is looking, without covering what they need next. Prevent errors before explaining them. Confirm only what can't be undone, and say in the confirmation what will happen, to what, for how much; prefer undo. An error says what happened and how to put it right, next to its cause.
- **Every real state is designed.** Empty, loading, partial, failed, a limited role: each shows what it means and what the person can do, in the same structure as the normal state. A state no path of the product, with this change, can produce (its users, providers, jobs and migrations), shown so from the code at `file:line` and grounded, is a stated limit: not designed or pictured, only guarded to stop safely and visibly if it occurs. Missing data is not that evidence; without it the state is designed. Long values, many items, rounding, each language and direction are conditions every state meets, not states of their own.
- **It belongs to the product.** Use the design system's tokens and components and the patterns the product already uses for this kind of screen. A new pattern is a product decision, made only when none fits.
- **Everyone can use it, at every size.** Readable contrast, meaning never carried by colour alone, a sensible focus and reading order, full keyboard use, comfortable touch targets, layout that mirrors for right-to-left languages. At narrow widths the order follows importance: the answer and the primary action stay within reach, and the layout reflows rather than shrinks. Motion only shows continuity or a change of state, and respects reduced-motion settings.

## Practice

- **No pictures before the build, except for a new screen.** A genuinely new screen, or one the person rejected, goes to the person as a memo of two or three directions that differ in structure (the focal point, the groups, where actions live and how they rank), described in words; never a static mock. Every other visible change is built and judged on the built screen.
- **Have it read once, built.** A fresh reader, given only the built screenshots and the purpose with its five to eight questions, answers each yes or no per pictured state, saying where on the screen and whether an element or only a sentence answered it. Its "no"s go to review, which confirms or refutes each on the running screen; what it had to work out is a note for the handoff.
- **Judge in passes, from the rendered screen.** First glance: what is seen first, and is it what the person most needs? Squint: does the hierarchy hold when the words blur? Cover the sentences: can the person still tell what is true and what to do? Read: can each of the person's questions be answered from the screen, and does everything on it tell one story? Detail: consistency, alignment, type, spacing, words and every real state. Judge against the product's best screens of the same kind and recognised practice for that kind of screen, never against the nearest page. Be strict: "acceptable" is not the bar, and a screenshot or the code is not the live screen.
- **After the person rejects a screen,** restate its purpose with them, then derive its directions again from that purpose, not from the findings against the last version.

The project supplies its specifics: its design system and where it lives, its components, its best reference screen for each kind of surface, and the widths, languages and themes its states need. They come from its `AGENTS.md` and its project definition.

These principles guide the planner and the implementer. Review judges the built screen by its rubric, not by walking this list.
