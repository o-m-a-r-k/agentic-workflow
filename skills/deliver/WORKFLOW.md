# The way of working

The goal: a ticket goes in, and the outcome it exists for comes true for its users, right and as soon as it can be done right, with no impact nobody saw coming. The person sees the plan. They are asked only to approve high-risk plans and to make product decisions.

## The outcome

Everything else in this method serves this section.

- **Every ticket is for an outcome.** It is what is true in its users' world once the ticket is delivered, not the code, the tests or the steps: the failing request now completes for the customer who hit it; the operator settles a held payment correctly without asking anyone; the new report gives the totals its readers act on. Before anything else, write down the outcome, the most direct evidence of it (where it can be seen to be true: the users' own data and logs, read-only; the screen as they meet it; the money the feature produces), and how soon it is needed.
- **The orchestrator owns the distance to it.** Each cycle, `PROGRESS.md` says what is now known about the outcome, with its evidence, and what still stands between the users and it. The next move is the one that most shortens that distance, or removes the largest uncertainty about it, for its cost. The steps below are the usual moves in their usual order; the outcome decides how deep each goes and what else is worth doing, never that grounding, review, verification, a high-risk approval or the delivery checks are skipped.
- **Direct evidence first; stand-ins only while they track it.** Get the outcome's own evidence as early as it can be had. A local reproduction, a mocked state, a benchmark, a passing suite, a reader's answers and a reviewer's sign-off stand in for it, and count only while they are shown to move with it. When one doesn't (a local run that doesn't fail the way the users' did, a screen whose answers come from paragraphs, a gain too small to change what the users meet), it stops counting, and the work goes back to the outcome's own evidence. Making the stand-in stricter is not progress.
- **A move earns its place by what it could still reveal or change about the outcome,** weighed against the time it costs the people waiting for it. Depth that doesn't change the outcome is waste: an unlikely, cheap failure is an accepted risk with its numbers, and a state shown unreachable gets only the guard that keeps it safe. An outcome left wrong is never cheap: wrong money, data, permissions or state, once shown reachable through the product's real paths (its users, providers and jobs), however rare, is never an accepted risk. A rare failure on a path that moves money or sets things up is made to stop safely instead: stuck, visible and correctable by an operator, never wrong. Building automatic recovery for it is a decision memo for the person, with its cost.
- **Progress is the distance shrinking,** judged against when the outcome is needed: an item is gone from what `PROGRESS.md` says still stands between the users and the outcome, and none took its place. A finding, a revision or a passing run is not progress by itself. A round that didn't bring the users nearer, or whose fixes keep opening gaps of their own, is the signal to re-plan from the outcome, not to repeat the round, add a check, or file the rest away. When one re-plan hasn't brought it nearer by the next round, or the time it is needed by will pass first, stop and take the evidence and the options to the person, with a recommendation; the ticket waits for their answer. No option skips a step or accepts something wrong.
- **The users' part ships first.** The plan names the smallest change that makes the outcome true for the users waiting, proven and leaving no wrong money, data, permissions or state reachable through what it changes or restores, and shows it can be delivered alone or says why not. When the related work beyond it is large, the users' part is delivered as soon as it is proven and the rest follows as this ticket's next delivery; otherwise it is one delivery. A proven users' part is held back only by a regression the change caused or by what it can't be true without.

## How the work is trusted

- **Agents check agents.** Confidence comes from a fresh agent checking the work against raw sources: code, git, logs, data, the running app, and for a screen, how it reads to the person using it. It never comes from the author's own account. A check that saw only a screenshot or the code says so.
- **Grounded.** Every claim names its source: `file:line`, a read-only query and its result, a log line, a screenshot, the ticket, or an authoritative external source with its date. A design choice names the constraints it meets and the alternatives it beats. A claim without a source goes back to investigation, never forward.
- **Findings are claims too.** Whoever receives a finding checks it before acting on it. A false finding is disputed with evidence, and the next reviewer settles it. A finding counts by what it does to the outcome or to what already works; matters of taste are not findings.
- **Prove by running.** A question the design hinges on is settled by running something before anything is built on it: a query, a throwaway spike in the worktree (a picture's spike is kept as a patch, as the `design` skill says), the screen in a browser. Whoever writes a test runs it. Whatever the project's environments can produce (a state, an account, data, a running app to walk through) is produced and checked, never reported as unchecked or a limit; only what can't be produced there, such as a real third party, real money or elapsed time, is reported, first, as work not done, with why.
- **Proof belongs to a version.** Grounding and approval name the plan revision, tests and review the commit, verification the build. When what was proven changes, the proof that rested on it no longer counts, and a fresh agent does it again for what changed; a base update proves again only what it changed in what this change alters or relies on, and leaves every other proof standing.
- **Meaning first.** What a person sees, a screen or a report, exists so they understand and can act. The plugin's `design` skill holds the goals, principles and practice for it; what a screen shows or says is a claim, true in every real state that shows it.
- **Files, not memory.** The ticket folder is the state. Every cycle starts by reading it again; where the files and anyone's recollection disagree, the files win.

## Scope

The outcome sets the scope: that outcome as its users meet it, in every real state (for a bug or hotfix, the behaviour it restores, not how the product met the failure), not only the lines this change writes. Related work is the ticket's: whatever the users can't get the outcome without, whoever wrote it, and whatever the change caused, fixed at its root as far as it reaches. A guarantee wider than the outcome is named in the plan with where it holds; what the feature does where it fails is related. Everything else found on the way is extra, even in the same screen or code, even when it is wrong, and including making that guarantee hold everywhere or redesigning shared machinery. Extras are recorded under "Outside the ticket" in `PROGRESS.md` and shown to the person, one at a time or several together, each explained in plain words (what is wrong, who meets it, what fixing it would take) with a recommendation: file it, fix it now, or fix it after this delivery; a hotfix's are shown after its first delivery. Work on the outcome goes on while they decide. What they choose to fix now joins the plan in the delivery it gives it; what they choose to fix after becomes this ticket's next delivery. Wrong money, data, permissions or state reaching users now is told to the person at once. Deferring never makes the outcome smaller: what the users can't get it without stays this ticket's work.

The project's `AGENTS.md` files and the person's own instructions add to this method; where they ask for more, they win. They never remove a step: authority to deliver doesn't approve a high-risk plan. Only the person, of their own accord in the session, can waive a step, for the ticket they name; never ask them to waive one; delivering the users' part first, as "The outcome" describes, is not a waiver.

## Ticket types and size

The type says what kind of outcome it is; size says how much stands between the users and it. Every fresh agent pays to read the same code again, so a small ticket uses few: one investigates and plans in the same breath, and one fresh agent grounds and challenges it in a single pass. A larger ticket gets its lanes and separate roles. Whatever the size, the one who checks is never the one who wrote, every plan is grounded, and every change is reviewed and verified.

- **Hotfix:** the people hitting a production failure work again, now, as they did before it, and its cause is removed, at its root as far as it reaches; nothing else is its outcome, even in the same screen or code. Getting them working again is its users' part; where the cause can still reach users, it is guarded so it fails safe, never with wrong money, data, permissions or state. Removing the cause follows as the next delivery when it takes longer.
- **Bug:** the failing behaviour works again as it did, or as it was meant to where it never did, with its cause removed, without the urgency. For both, the outcome's evidence is that behaviour measured against where it worked: the version before the change that broke it, found from history, or what it was meant to do. A change that leaves the cause in place and hides, retries or recovers from the failure, or alters what the users relied on before it broke, doesn't shorten that distance: apart from the hotfix's guard, it lies outside the ticket. Where the cause can't be removed without such a change, that is a decision for the person, with undoing the change that broke it among the options.
- **Enhancement:** behaviour that exists changes as asked, and everything that relies on it keeps working.
- **New feature:** its users can do the new thing, correctly, in every real state. A complex one needs architecture, design, several angles and its edge cases worked out.

The person names the type in the session, unless the project definition says where else it comes from; otherwise infer it from the ticket.

## The steps

1. **Take the ticket.** Choose its delivery target as the project definition says; a hotfix's is the one production is built from. Create the ticket folder `<work>/<ticket>/` from the templates, and inside it a worktree per repo the ticket needs, each branched from that repo's freshly fetched base for the target and set up as the definition says. If the work folder lies inside a repo, make sure that repo's `.git/info/exclude` lists it. Move the ticket to its `started` status. Write the outcome, its evidence and its urgency into `PROGRESS.md`, and tell the person in one line the outcome, type, size and the moves you expect, so they can correct them. Lay out the lanes: what runs together, what each writes, and where they merge.

2. **Understand.** Send investigators, one angle each, side by side where the settings allow.
   - *Bug or hotfix:* see the failure where it happens, as far as reading allows: logs, traces, read-only data and queries, the screen as its user sees it. That is the outcome's own evidence, and the cause is found from it. Anything that would write there is reproduced locally, with simulated data shaped like production, never copied from it.
   - *Enhancement:* the current behaviour, with sources; every consumer (other screens, APIs, reports, exports, integrations); the existing data that must keep working; the ticket's open questions.
   - *New feature:* the system it plugs into (architecture, conventions, data model, permissions and tenancy, design system, real data volumes); the domain, from authoritative sources; prior art.

   Whatever the ticket, walk the users' path where they meet it before planning, in the target environment or the closest the project can produce, reading only; anything that would write there is walked locally. The orchestrator asks the person at once for every access that needs. Find the reach of what the change alters: what uses it, through code, shared data, events or contracts, and what elsewhere does the same job and must stay consistent with it. Each part of that reach the change touches is proven still working, by its own tests or by a check the plan adds. Investigation ends when the questions the outcome hinges on are answered.

3. **Plan.** Write `PLAN.md` and `CHECKS.json`. The plan says how the change reaches the outcome and the evidence that it will: that the change is enough to reach the outcome, not only that it improves something. It holds the decisions with their evidence and the alternatives they beat, a test for each behaviour and edge case, the checks that will show the outcome, the accepted risks and what lies outside. It is as long as its decisions need. A change people will see is proven as a picture and read fresh before it is built, as the `design` skill describes. A product question becomes a decision memo for the person: the options, shown where they can be seen, the market and competitors, the industry standard, and a recommendation. The plan names its deliveries, the users' part first, and which checks each is due before. A changed decision raises its revision; a fact that changes no decision, and logistics such as runner flags, worktrees, scheduling or the plan's own wording (words people will see are design, revised and grounded), go in `PROGRESS.md`'s log, not the plan. A plan is written on the code it will be built on: each revision names the commit of each worktree it read, and while it is written, grounded and challenged no one else changes the files its new or changed claims rest on; a fix due in those files waits for the revision or joins it. Work on other files goes on. Work for an earlier delivery, and a base update, never wait for a later delivery's plan; the planner reads what they changed, and the grounder checks the revision against it.

4. **Ground and challenge,** on the same revision, side by side where the settings allow. A fresh grounder checks each claim against its source, by running something wherever it can. A fresh challenger assumes the plan was carried out exactly and the outcome still didn't come true, or something that worked broke, and explains why. The planner folds both in. A changed decision is a new revision; its changed claims are grounded again, and the challenge is asked again only if the design changed. A corrected claim that changes no decision is a new revision grounded for that claim alone, never challenged. The plan's own wording and logistics go in `PROGRESS.md`'s log.

5. **Judge risk.** How much harm could this change do if it were wrong? The planner and the grounder each judge it from reach, reversibility and remaining uncertainty, with evidence. If they disagree, the higher level stands.
   - **Low:** work goes on. The person can read the plan at any time.
   - **High:** pause for the person's approval, asking once: show the plan and any decision memos as a visual page, link the plan file, and record their words, the time and the revision in `PROGRESS.md`. A later revision that changes the design, the scope or the risk needs their approval again.

6. **Build.** A fresh implementer takes the plan as its brief. Independent items run side by side where the settings allow, each in its own worktree merged back in order; items that share files run in turn. The implementer runs every test it writes or changes and the project's fast suite before it calls the work done. A new fact that changes a decision goes into the plan with its evidence; if it raises the risk to high, the plan goes to the person; if the plan turns out wrong at its root, return to planning instead of patching around it.

7. **Review.** Every change gets one. A fresh reviewer reads the whole change against the plan and the outcome: does it reach the outcome, is every planned edge case handled and tested, does it keep the project's rules and design system, and does it reach anything outside the scope? Where the change alters what people see, a fresh reader on that commit's screenshots comes first, as the `design` skill describes. If the reviewer judges the change needs more confidence, it says what, and that is done.

8. **Verify** on the commit to be delivered, in the target environment as the project defines it: the outcome's own evidence wherever it can be seen before delivery, the real flow in a browser, data checks, screenshots where screens are affected, and the proofs that cover what the change alters and its reach. Proof is the tests that reach covers, never a complete suite by habit: a complete suite runs only where the reach can't be named (dependencies, build or test tooling, shared infrastructure), once per delivery, and a repair reruns only the tests that cover it. Run each once, on the tree to be delivered; a later base update sends back only the checks whose proof it touched. A check flips to passing only after it is seen to pass. Any check that fails on that commit is this ticket's to fix, even in code the change didn't touch, except a failure the change didn't cause, shown by failing the same way on the base, and that the outcome doesn't need, which is an extra as Scope says; otherwise a delivery never stands on a red or flaky run.

9. **Deliver** to the ticket's target, as the project defines it. If the base has moved and the project's way of delivering needs the branch current, first bring it up to date that way; only what the update changed in what this change alters or relies on is proven again. Before each irreversible step (merge, deploy, ticket move), a fresh delivery agent checks from raw sources that every proof belongs to what it would deliver, takes the step, and confirms it from its output. The outcome is then checked where it lives: the users' data and logs, the live screen. A delivery whose outcome doesn't appear isn't delivered: roll back what broke or made it worse, otherwise re-plan, and tell the person; where only time or real use can show the outcome, the handoff says what will show it and when. The handoff and the report open with the outcome and the evidence it is now true, then what changed, in plain words; what it reaches and how that was proven to keep working; the screenshots of the states "What people see" pictures; the extras found along the way and what the person chose for each; the risks accepted; and where it lives. Each delivery posts its handoff; the ticket moves to `delivered` with its last. **Screenshots are a gate:** when the change affects what people see, the handoff carries the screenshots of every state "What people see" pictures, of the delivered commit, or of an earlier commit for the pictures whose words and layout nothing since has changed, attached in the tracker, read back and matched, before the ticket moves to `delivered`.

10. **Clean up**, once the last delivery is made, leaving the project as it was apart from the delivery: canonical checkouts on the delivered commit; worktrees, merged branches, whatever `PROGRESS.md` lists as left behind or the project definition's way of finding leftovers shows, and the ticket folder removed, each confirmed from its output. Then, where `~/.agents` already existed when the ticket started, append one line to `~/.agents/records.jsonl`.

## Several tickets

Each ticket is independent and is delivered when it is ready. Nothing waits for a shared gate, and a pause for one ticket never pauses another. A heavy test run starts when the free memory measured where it will run covers its peak with a margin, after taking off, for every heavy run already going there (whoever started it), what it has yet to reach of its own peak. A run makes itself visible to other sessions before it takes memory, and the measurement is repeated just before it starts; otherwise it waits for room, never skipped. Where runs live in a container VM whose memory the machine backs on demand, both the VM and the machine must cover the run. The project definition says which runs are heavy, the peak of each, the margin, where to measure, how a starting run makes itself visible and how to see the runs going. A heavy run with no recorded peak starts only when no other heavy run is going there, and its measured peak goes into the evidence for the definition. `heavy_runs`, where the user settings set it, is an outer limit on how many run at once. The most urgent outcome goes first.

## Learning

The records, their `missed` lists, the handoffs and the reopened tickets show where the method fails. A kind of miss that repeats points at the method: find what in it let the work drift from the outcome, and change that, rather than adding a clause for the case. Learning is its own piece of work, never done inside a ticket or in the installed copy: a change to this method's repository, or to the project's definition or `AGENTS.md`.
