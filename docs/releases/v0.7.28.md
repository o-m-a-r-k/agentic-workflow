## 0.7.28 - Fewer avoidable review rounds: the strategist and one planned pass; your exact answer counts as the agent showed it

- **The strategist is the next step when the course drifts, not a call the agent can skip (I-210, I-208, D44).**
  Named failure: in a three-ticket batch (35 hours, 47 review rounds against 11 planned) the agent running the tickets
  wrote "converging, not calling the strategist" six times and handed it no work, while the changed files ran two to four
  times what the plan expected and the criteria were raised four times. Now the workflow names six signals itself:
  - files changed past twice what the plan (or the latest re-plan or strategist) expected;
  - review rounds past what it expected;
  - criteria added after implementation started;
  - a problem whose cause came back after a fix;
  - a round whose findings sit on the lines the last fix wrote (the fix made new defects);
  - an implementer that expects far more time than the fix the reviewer described (more than twice it, and by more than the
    plan gives a whole review round, so two small estimates apart are not sent to the strategist), or that asks for a re-plan.

  While one stands that the strategist has not answered, the next fix round, planned pass or code review is replaced by
  the strategist: `wf resume` says so, names the signals and offers no "keep going" beside them. The strategist's
  decision drives what comes next: one planned pass for all the remaining work, a split, or "keep going" with a new
  expectation the course is then measured against. It is an agent's step, never a question to you; nothing is refused,
  and a gate, an evidence review, the acceptance and delivery are never held for it. When an implementer is still at work,
  the step says the strategist decides alongside and that a decision changing the approach stops it first. The re-plan an
  implementer itself asked for is not counted as a drift. A project that switched the strategist off gets none of it.
- **The ten-round question has an answer for "this needs a product or legal decision" (I-210).** Named failure: you
  answered a round-10 question with a question of your own ("what is the industry best practice here?"); no option fitted
  and it stayed open 390 minutes overnight. A fifth option, "It needs a product or legal answer first: hold", holds the
  ticket in those words until you lift it.
- **Small findings and the items an implementer leaves are fixed in one planned pass, not a round each (I-207, D45).**
  Named failure: ten of one ticket's sixteen review rounds were about low findings or nothing at all, because each low
  finding and each item an implementer left behind became its own fix round and its own review round, and your standing
  "fix all" was used to open each one. "Fix everything" stays your rule; what changed is how:
  - an item an implementer leaves no longer stops the work before the review;
  - while there are real problems to fix, the small ones are fixed in that same round;
  - when only small ones are left, they are planned as one pass with its own time estimate, built by one implementer, and
    checked by the review that follows the test run anyway, with no extra review round; that review is told the pass was
    built, which items it fixed and where, since it is the only review the pass gets;
  - when you say "fix all", the agent records it once (`wf scope fix-all`), and only with your own words: the workflow
    checks they are in one of your messages in that session (or you run it in your own terminal and type yes). You are not
    asked about each item again, also for items your project lets the agent decide, and if you later want one left out,
    you say so and only that one is asked.
- **A fix stays as small as the problem; a redesign goes back to planning (I-208, D46).** Named failure: a reviewer
  described a narrow fix ("compute the fingerprint before the provider call") and the implementer built a 240-minute
  redesign instead; it caused the batch's only serious finding and four new ones. Reviewers now say the narrowest fix and
  roughly how long it takes; the implementer fixes with the smallest change that removes the cause, and a fix that needs a
  database migration, a new state or a new endpoint is sent back as a re-plan request instead of being built in a fix
  round. Planners name every place a repeated pattern lives as one sweep, so reviewers stop finding the same cause one place
  per round, and a re-plan restates all the remaining work as one pass.
- **The history of earlier findings reaches every fix, whatever release started the ticket (I-209).** Named failure: the
  batch's fix rounds never got the history 0.7.27 added (where each earlier finding stands and what fixed it), because
  their findings did not block and the tickets had been started under 0.7.17 and 0.7.18; the same cause was then found one
  place per round. Information that only helps the implementer now reaches every ticket in flight.
- **A divided review can only be started the way that records it (I-211, D48).** Named failure: a review split between
  several reviewers was started as one plain reviewer; its parts were never created and the agent waited about 80 minutes
  for nothing. Such a review now prints only the command that starts it, a reviewer started any other way stops at its
  first step, and `wf resume` says nothing is running.
- **A batch member runs its own new end-to-end tests, and a test suite that was failing before can be set aside by you
  (I-212, D47).** Named failure: with the long test steps left for the batch, a ticket's own new end-to-end test first ran
  at hour 28 and no reviewer saw its screenshots before then; the batch's test run then failed five times (158 minutes) on
  tests that were already flaky and that the batch never touched, each failure becoming a repair round. A step can now
  name its test files, and a member runs the ones its change adds or edits. When a suite fails whose test file the delivery does
  not edit, `wf resume` names it with the way to ask you, and you get one question: set it aside for this delivery, or fix
  it here. The question says only what the workflow checked (the test file is not edited) and lists the files the change
  edits that the tests read, since a change to the code a test exercises can be what fails it; the agent's reason is shown
  as the agent's. Set aside, the test run passes on it and the delivered comment says it was set aside and what was not
  checked.
- **An amendment file that is not there, or a survey put in the wrong place, gets a plain answer (I-213, D48).** Named
  failure: `wf criteria amend` given a path where no file was printed the engine's stack trace, and the advice for where a
  survey goes sent the agent between two refusals; it read the engine's source to find the way. Every command now says an
  input is not there before reading it, and an amendment is told that its survey goes under `impact`.

- **A panel option written with its description is still that option (I-202, D43).** Named failure: you answered "Use
  the corrected step" in Codex's question panel. The agent had written each option as its label with what follows it
  ("Use the corrected step (Recommended): the corrected step replaces it …"), because that panel takes plain strings,
  so the workflow did not see the decision's options and reported no answer; the agent's chat message had put the
  question in bold and typographic quotes, so the chat route did not match either. Now:
  - an option that is one of the decision's labels, followed at most by " (Recommended)" (or "(Not recommended)") and
    ": <what follows>" (or a dash), counts as that label, and your pick binds by itself, whether the app records the
    whole option or only the label; this is a match on the label at the option's start, never a reading of the rest:
    "Use the corrected step and skip the review" is not one of the options, and neither is one whose description names
    the other option ("yes: Keep it paused"), which the agent reads instead;
  - the exact question still binds when the agent shows it in bold, italics, code marks, a quote block, typographic
    quotes or Markdown escapes (`wait\_for\_qa`), or across lines; every check that it asked nothing else and no other
    decision reads the same text;
  - when your words do not bind by themselves, the refusal says what did not match (the panel's options beside the
    decision's, a paraphrased question, something else asked in the same message) and tells the agent to read your
    words, your answer in the panel included, which it quotes like a typed reply; it never says there was no answer,
    and the question is never put to you again;
  - each app's note says how its panel takes options, wherever the panel is offered: each option is the label alone,
    and what follows it goes in its description, or, in Codex's `request_user_input_async`, in the question's text.
- **A question an older release asked, with a "?" added, takes your bare "yes" (I-203).** From the review of 0.7.27: an
  agent wrote such a question with a "?" at the end, and your "yes" was refused in the chat and in the panel as if the
  message had asked something else. A "?" right after the question is now part of it when the question starts its own
  sentence; one that ends a sentence shared with another ask ("Shall I delete the branch, and <the question>?") still
  means something else was asked, as does one anywhere else. A panel that offers such a question's labels ("Close it
  as shipped" / "Keep it open") binds your pick, and a refusal about a panel question names the panel, not "your
  message".
- **Your later "yes" after an explanation decides (I-174).** An older report: you asked what a question meant, got the
  explanation, then answered "yes", and the clarification was recorded instead. Since 0.7.27 the workflow records by
  itself only an exact answer, and your latest reply to the question decides: a "yes" to the question asked again is
  recorded, and a "yes" after an explanation that did not ask it again is the agent's to read and record, never the
  clarification. A scenario keeps this as the proof.
